Start Talking About F*cking Money

August 1st, 2026

We need to start talking about money.

Not occasionally. Not apologetically. Not only when a funding round closes, a mortgage application is rejected, or an organisation is three months away from running out of cash.

We need to talk about how money works, who has it, where it comes from, where it goes, how it compounds, and how it quietly determines who gets to shape the world.

Over the past year, I have been diving deeper into finance and economics, exploring the social-investment market, speaking with charities, founders, professors, bankers, social ventures, fundraising consultants, and attending a social venture weekend at Cambridge University, and reflecting on my own experience working in financial technology (which I very much enjoy!)

I have met some truly amazing and inspiring people.

People supporting those experiencing homelessness. People improving education. People protecting nature, strengthening communities, and trying to repair systems that routinely fail the people who depend on them.

Their missions are serious and valuable. Their commitment is real. Their ideas are often excellent.

But I keep coming away with the uncomfortable feeling that the system surrounding them is completely missing the point.

We can talk for hours about mission, values, community, impact and purpose. We can discuss theories of change, beneficiary engagement, reporting frameworks, and moving case studies.

But ask who pays, who profits, who carries the risk, who owns the asset, how much somebody earns, or how the organisation will ever build financial strength of its own, and the temperature in the room changes.

A lot of people, particularly in England and in the social sector, appear almost allergic to money.

We don’t like talking about it. We often don’t understand it. We are afraid of appearing greedy, crude or self-interested.

Money is apparently too vulgar for polite conversation.

Meanwhile, the wealthiest and most powerful people in society never stop thinking about it.

Entire institutions and careers are dedicated to capital allocation, tax structures, ownership, leverage, property, equity, inheritance and returns. Armies of analysts, lawyers and investment managers examine these subjects in scientific detail.

Wealthy families teach their children how the system works. They accumulate assets, relationships, knowledge and influence over generations.

Unsurprisingly, they get to shape society.

Their children go to good schools. They enter strong professional networks. They receive help buying homes. They can take unpaid internships, start companies, withstand failure and wait for the right opportunities. They move into institutions that influence policy, investment, business and culture.

And the rest of us are told that money is a little distasteful to discuss.

How are we supposed to make improvements if we refuse to learn the game through which power is distributed?

Silence is not neutrality

There is an uncomfortable parallel here with the way societies have historically treated religion and sexuality.

Don’t talk about it. Don’t ask questions. Don’t look too closely.

It is private, improper or forbidden.

Except, of course, for the people already using it.

Silence is not neutral. Silence protects the people who already understand the system.

When ordinary people are uncomfortable discussing salaries, employers gain an advantage.

When charities are uncomfortable discussing their real operating costs, funders gain an advantage.

When founders are uncomfortable discussing ownership and investment terms, investors gain an advantage.

When families are uncomfortable discussing inheritance, debt and property, inequality reproduces itself quietly.

When socially motivated people treat profit as morally suspicious, people with no such discomfort are left to decide what gets funded, built and owned.

Avoiding conversations about money does not make money less powerful. It makes its power less visible.

That invisibility is useful to someone.

In my experience, it is rarely the people trying to improve their communities.

I learned enough of the game to participate

I now earn, roughly every two years, what it took my parents their entire working lives to save.

That is an uncomfortable sentence to write.

It is not because I am a genius. It is not because I work thirty hours a day. It is not because I stopped buying avocado toast, developed superhuman discipline or possess some extraordinary moral virtue.

I entered an industry that pays well.

I learned how salaries, equity and negotiation work. I understood which skills the market rewards. I moved countries. I changed jobs when the economics made sense.

I learned enough of the game to participate in it.

None of this is magical. It is not even especially complicated.

But it is exclusive.

You have to know that the game exists. You have to believe that you are allowed to play it. You need exposure to people who understand the rules, enough safety to take risks, and enough confidence to ask uncomfortable questions.

That knowledge is not distributed equally.

Some people grow up discussing investments, tax, property, business ownership and inheritance around the dinner table.

Others grow up believing that investing is gambling, negotiating is rude, debt is always irresponsible and wealthy people became wealthy simply by working harder.

This is not a difference in intelligence. It is a difference in exposure.

The social sector cannot afford financial innocence

This problem becomes particularly severe in charities and social ventures.

People enter the sector because they care. They want to support vulnerable people, improve communities, protect nature or address problems the market and state have failed to solve.

That commitment is beyond admirable.

It is also, unfortunately, routinely exploited.

Organisations accept underfunded contracts because the alternative is abandoning people who need them.

Staff work beyond their paid hours because the work matters.

Founders underpay themselves because discussing their own financial needs feels selfish.

Funders pay for visible programme delivery but resist paying for management, technology, pensions, reserves, evaluation and organisational development.

The resulting gap is filled by sacrifice.

Moral commitment becomes an unrecorded source of subsidy.

We call it passion, dedication, going above and beyond.

But financially, somebody is absorbing a cost that another institution has chosen not to pay.

Often it is the employee. Sometimes it is the founder. Sometimes it is their family. Sometimes it is the organisation’s future stability.

This is where I think the system is missing the point.

We keep asking mission-driven organisations to prove their impact while denying them the financial structures required to become durable, capable institutions.

We celebrate programmes but distrust overhead. We finance activity but not resilience. We ask organisations to innovate while keeping them dependent on short-term grants, restricted funds, delayed payments and endless applications.

We praise founders for their sacrifice rather than asking why sacrifice was required in the first place.

A social enterprise is not more virtuous because its employees are underpaid.

A charity is not more impactful because it has no reserves.

A founder is not more committed because they cannot afford to start a family.

An organisation is not efficient because its essential infrastructure is being held together through exhaustion and unpaid labour.

These are not signs of moral seriousness.

They are signs that somebody else is receiving a subsidy.

Mission is not a substitute for a financial model

The conversations I have been having have made me increasingly convinced that we separate mission and money far too easily.

Mission is treated as the real work.

Money is treated as the slightly embarrassing administrative work required to keep it going.

But finance determines what the organisation can actually do.

It determines whether it can hire good people, invest in technology, survive a delayed payment, negotiate a bad contract, expand a successful programme or walk away from a funder whose demands are unreasonable.

A financial model is not something that sits beneath the mission. It shapes the mission’s range of possible outcomes.

That does not mean every charity should become a profit-maximising company. It does not mean every social problem can or should produce a commercial return.

It means we should stop pretending that good intentions can compensate indefinitely for badly designed financial structures.

Money is not the same as value

Money is not the only thing that matters. Those who chase it exclusively are often miserable.

Money is not connection, dignity, health, community, trust, or meaning. But money is a claim on resources.

It affects who can access land, housing, labour, materials, education, time, expertise, security and political attention.

It decides which ideas survive long enough to become institutions.

It determines who can experiment, who can recover from failure and who has to accept the first available option.

Money is not identical to power, but it can be converted into power with extraordinary reliability.

Pretending otherwise does not make us morally superior.

It makes us strategically ineffective.

Profit is not the enemy

People working on social problems also need to become more precise about profit.

Profit can absolutely be exploitative. It can come from monopoly, extraction, environmental destruction, underpaid labour, regulatory capture or artificial scarcity.

But profit can also be evidence that an organisation has created something useful and found a sustainable way to provide it.

Without some form of surplus, an organisation cannot build reserves, invest in technology, train staff, withstand shocks, or expand.

The question is not simply whether somebody is making money.

The questions are: Why are they making it? What value did they create? What risk did they take? Who bears the costs? Who owns the resulting assets? Where does the surplus go? What behaviour does the structure encourage?

These are financial questions. They are also moral and political questions.

Refusing to discuss profit does not prevent extraction. It prevents us from distinguishing healthy value creation from exploitation.

Learn the machinery

People who want to improve their communities need to understand the machinery through which society is organised.

That means learning about budgets, contracts, lending, taxation, investment, ownership, procurement, insurance, property, pensions and capital markets.

It means asking uncomfortable questions.

Why is this socially essential service funded through a short-term grant rather than a permanent budget?

Why does the smallest supplier carry the greatest cash-flow risk?

Why will a funder pay for delivery but not the infrastructure required to deliver it well?

Why are employees expected to accept lower pay because their work is socially useful?

Who owns the assets?

Who carries the downside?

Who captures the upside?

These questions are not distractions from social impact.

They are the substance of it.

Housing, transport, healthcare, childcare, energy and education are not only policy problems. They are systems of ownership, financing, incentives and risk allocation.

You cannot meaningfully reform them without understanding where the money flows.

Talk about fucking money

So talk about your salary.

Talk about your rent, debt, savings, pension, equity and inheritance.

Ask founders who owns their company.

Ask charities what their work actually costs.

Ask funders why they will finance delivery but not organisational capacity.

Ask employers for the salary range before attending five rounds of interviews.

Ask what happens to the profits.

Ask who carries the downside.

Ask who owns the upside.

Teach children what shares are. Explain compound interest. Show them a payslip. Explain tax, mortgages and pensions. Tell them that negotiating is not rude and financial literacy is not greed.

Learn how the system works, and then teach somebody else.

Because the people who already have money are talking about it.

They are planning.

They are investing.

They are organising.

They are passing knowledge and assets to the next generation.

They are using money to shape institutions, markets, neighbourhoods and governments.

Those of us who want a fairer society cannot continue treating money as an embarrassing administrative detail.

We need to understand it.

We need to discuss it openly.

We need to become competent at using it.

And we need to stop leaving one of the most powerful tools in society entirely in the hands of people whose vision for the world we may not share.

Start talking about fucking money.
