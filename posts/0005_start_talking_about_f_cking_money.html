Start Talking About F*cking Money

August 1st, 2026

We need to start talking about money.

Not only when a funding round closes, a mortgage application is rejected, or an organisation is three months away from running out of cash.

We need to talk about how money works, who has it, where it comes from, where it goes, how it compounds, and how it quietly determines who gets to shape the world.

Over the past year, I have been diving deeper into finance and economics, exploring the social-investment market, speaking with charities, founders, professors, bankers, social ventures and fundraising consultants, attending a social venture weekend at Cambridge University (which I loved!) and reflecting on my own experience working in financial technology (which I also love!).

I have met some truly amazing and inspiring people.

People supporting those experiencing homelessness. People improving education. People protecting nature, strengthening communities and trying to repair systems that routinely fail the people who depend on them.

Their missions are serious and valuable. Their commitment is real. Their ideas are often excellent.

But I keep coming away with an uncomfortable feeling that the system surrounding them is missing something fundamental.

We can talk for hours about mission, values, community, impact and purpose. We can discuss theories of change, beneficiary engagement, reporting frameworks and moving case studies.

But ask who pays, who profits, who carries the risk, who owns the asset, how much somebody earns, or how an organisation will ever build financial strength of its own, and the conversation often becomes more difficult.

A lot of us, particularly in England and in the social sector, seem deeply uncomfortable talking about money.

We may understand the immediate financial reality painfully well: what salaries cost, how long a grant will last, what happens when a payment arrives late.

But discussing money as a source of power, agency, resilience or institutional strength can still feel greedy, crude or self-interested.

Money is treated as something slightly vulgar. Necessary, perhaps, but not something respectable people should examine too closely.

Meanwhile, many of the wealthiest and most powerful people in society treat money as a subject worthy of constant study.

Entire institutions and careers are dedicated to capital allocation, tax structures, ownership, leverage, property, equity, inheritance and returns. Analysts, lawyers and investment managers examine these subjects in extraordinary detail.

Wealthy families pass that knowledge on - why wouldn’t they? They accumulate assets, relationships, experience and influence over generations.

Unsurprisingly, they are well placed to shape society.

Their children may attend good schools, enter strong professional networks, receive help buying homes, take unpaid internships, start companies, withstand failure and wait for the right opportunities.

They can move into institutions that influence policy, investment, business, and culture.

And the rest of us are often taught that talking about money is a little distasteful.

How are we supposed to improve society if we refuse to understand one of the primary systems through which power is distributed?

Silence is not neutrality

There is a familiar pattern here.

Label a subject private, improper or vulgar, and the people who are already fluent in it retain an enormous advantage.

Silence is not neutral.

When people are uncomfortable discussing salaries, employers often know more than employees.

When charities cannot speak honestly about their full operating costs or get unrestricted funding for them, funders retain more power over what counts as legitimate expenditure.

When founders do not understand ownership and investment terms, investors arrive at the conversation better prepared.

When families avoid discussing inheritance, debt and property, inequality can reproduce itself quietly.

When socially motivated people treat profit as inherently suspicious, people with fewer moral concerns about money are left to decide what gets funded, built and owned.

Avoiding conversations about money does not make money less powerful.

It makes its power less visible.

And in my experience, that invisibility rarely serves the people trying to improve their communities.

I learned enough of the game to participate

I now earn, roughly every two years, what it took my parents their entire working lives to save.

That is an uncomfortable sentence to write.

It is not because I am a genius. It is not because I work thirty hours a day. It is not because I stopped buying avocado toast, developed superhuman discipline or possess some extraordinary moral virtue.

I entered an industry that pays well.

I learned how salaries, equity, and negotiation work. I understood which skills the market rewards. I moved countries. I changed jobs when the economics made sense.

I learned enough of the game to participate in it.

None of this is magical in inherently bad. Much of it is not even especially complicated once someone explains it.

But access to that explanation is not universal.

You have to know that the game exists. You have to believe that you are allowed to play it. You need exposure to people who understand the rules, enough safety to take risks and enough confidence to ask uncomfortable questions.

That knowledge is not distributed equally.

Some people grow up discussing investments, tax, property, business ownership and inheritance around the dinner table.

Others grow up believing that investing is gambling, negotiating is rude, debt is always irresponsible and wealthy people became wealthy simply by working harder.

This is not a difference in intelligence.

It is a difference in exposure, opportunity and permission.

The social sector cannot afford financial innocence

This problem becomes particularly severe in charities and social ventures.

People enter the sector because they driven by their mission for change. They want to support vulnerable people, improve communities, protect nature or address problems the market and state have failed to solve.

That commitment is beyond admirable.

It is also vulnerable to being exploited.

Organisations accept underfunded contracts because the alternative may be abandoning people who need them.

Staff work beyond their paid hours because the work matters.

Founders underpay themselves because discussing their own financial needs feels selfish.

Funders pay for visible programme delivery but can be reluctant to pay for management, technology, pensions, reserves, evaluation and organisational development.

The resulting gap is filled by sacrifice.

Moral commitment becomes an unrecorded source of subsidy.

We call it passion, dedication and going above and beyond.

But financially, somebody is absorbing a cost that another institution has chosen—or failed—to pay.

Often it is the employee. Sometimes it is the founder. Sometimes it is their family. Sometimes it is the organisation’s future stability.

This is where I think the system is missing the point.

We ask mission-driven organisations to prove their impact while frequently denying them the financial structures required to become durable, capable institutions.

We celebrate programmes but remain suspicious of overhead.

We finance activity more readily than resilience.

We ask organisations to innovate while keeping them dependent on short-term grants, restricted funds, delayed payments and repeated applications.

We praise founders for their sacrifice rather than asking why so much sacrifice was required in the first place.

A social enterprise is not necessarily more virtuous because its employees are underpaid.

A charity is not necessarily more impactful because it has no reserves.

A founder should not have to demonstrate commitment by making it impossible to support their own family.

An organisation is not truly efficient if its essential infrastructure is being held together through exhaustion and unpaid labour.

These may look like signs of dedication.

But they can also be signs that somebody else is receiving an invisible subsidy.

Mission is not a substitute for a financial model

The conversations I have been having have made me increasingly convinced that we separate mission and money far too easily.

Mission is treated as the real work.

Money is treated as the slightly embarrassing administrative work required to keep it going.

But finance determines what an organisation can actually do.

It determines whether it can hire and retain good people, invest in technology, survive a delayed payment, negotiate a bad contract, expand a successful programme or walk away from a funder whose demands are unreasonable.

A financial model is not something that sits beneath the mission.

It shapes the mission’s range of possible outcomes.

That does not mean every charity should become a profit-maximising company.

It does not mean every social problem can or should produce a commercial return.

It means we should stop expecting good intentions to compensate indefinitely for badly designed financial structures.

The people I have met are not short on purpose, intelligence, or commitment.

What they often lack is a system that converts the value they create into lasting financial strength.

An organisation may create enormous social value while remaining financially fragile.

It may save public institutions money without receiving any share of those savings.

It may improve a community while the resulting economic gains accrue to landlords, employers, investors or the state.

It may spend years developing expertise, relationships and trust, only to remain dependent on the next restricted grant.

The work succeeds.

The organisation remains precarious.

That is not only a fundraising problem.

It is a problem of how value is recognised, financed, captured and distributed.

Money is not the same as value

Money is far from the only thing that matters. Those who chase it exclusively are often miserable.

Money is not connection, dignity, health, community, trust, or meaning.

But money is a claim on resources.

It affects who can access land, housing, labour, materials, education, time, expertise, security, and political attention.

It helps determine which ideas survive long enough to become institutions.

It affects who can experiment, who can recover from failure and who has to accept the first available option.

Money is not identical to power, but it can be converted into power with extraordinary reliability.

Acknowledging that does not mean accepting that everything should be bought and sold.

It means recognising the terrain on which many of our social choices are already being made.

Ignoring that terrain does not make us morally superior.

It makes us less able to change it.

Profit is not the enemy

People working on social problems also need to become more precise about profit.

Profit can absolutely be exploitative. It can come from monopoly, extraction, environmental destruction, underpaid labour, regulatory capture or artificial scarcity.

But profit can also indicate that an organisation has created something useful and found a sustainable way to provide it.

Without some form of surplus, an organisation cannot build reserves, invest in technology, train staff, withstand shocks or expand.

The question is not simply whether somebody is making money.

The better questions are why are they making it? What value did they create? What risk did they take? Who bears the costs? Who owns the resulting assets? Where does the surplus go? What behaviour does the structure encourage?

These are financial questions.

They are also moral and political questions.

Refusing to discuss profit does not prevent extraction.

It makes it harder to distinguish sustainable value creation from exploitation.

Learn the machinery

People who want to improve their communities need to understand the machinery through which society is organised.

That means learning about budgets, contracts, lending, taxation, investment, ownership, procurement, insurance, property, pensions and capital markets.

It means becoming comfortable asking uncomfortable questions.

Why is this socially essential service funded through a short-term grant rather than a permanent budget?

Why does the smallest supplier carry the greatest cash-flow risk?

Why will a funder pay for delivery but not the infrastructure required to deliver it well?

Why are employees expected to accept lower pay because their work is socially useful?

Who owns the assets?

Who carries the downside?

Who captures the upside?

These questions are not distractions from social impact.

They are part of it.

Housing, transport, healthcare, childcare, energy and education are not only policy problems. They are also systems of ownership, financing, incentives and risk allocation.

We cannot meaningfully reform them without understanding where the money flows.

Talk about money

So talk about your salary.

Talk about your rent, debt, savings, pension, equity and inheritance.

Ask founders who owns their company.

Ask charities what their work actually costs.

Ask funders why they will finance delivery but not organisational capacity.

Ask employers for the salary range before attending five rounds of interviews.

Ask what happens to the profits.

Ask who carries the downside.

Ask who owns the upside.

Teach children what shares are. Explain compound interest. Show them a payslip. Explain tax, mortgages and pensions.

Tell them that negotiating is not rude and financial literacy is not greed.

Learn how the system works, and then teach somebody else.

Because the people who already have money are talking about it.

They are planning.

They are investing.

They are organising.

They are passing knowledge and assets to the next generation.

They are using money to shape institutions, markets, neighbourhoods and governments.

Those of us who want a fairer society cannot continue treating money as an embarrassing administrative detail.

We need to understand it.

We need to discuss it openly.

We need to become competent at using it.

And we need to stop leaving one of the most powerful tools in society entirely in the hands of people whose vision for the world we may not share.

Start talking about fucking money.
